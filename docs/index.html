The Intention of Major MIDI

Major MIDI is open source and collaborative by design. The work doesn’t end with me—it’s meant to be bent, tweaked, extended, and reinterpreted by the musicians, programmers, and builders who find possibilities in it I never anticipated.

It’s built on older technology, the way music itself builds on what came before. MIDI, General MIDI, and SoundFonts are an earlier era’s tools, but they’re still a useful shared vocabulary—one Major MIDI reinterprets rather than preserves as a fixed standard.

That vocabulary is the point of connection: Major MIDI brings MIDI’s structure and memory into a modular environment built on voltage, immediacy, and unpredictability—linking stored composition with live performance, MIDI gear with Eurorack, precise sequencing with hands-on control, without forcing either world to behave like the other.

Its physical interface reflects that goal. Nothing should hide behind a computer or force the musician to stop and configure something—the display and controls should make it feel like an instrument you explore and perform, not a utility you manage.

It’s also deliberately imperfect and unfinished: informed by General MIDI, but not bound to full compliance with it. It keeps what’s still musically useful and puts it inside a new, open-ended instrument—creative reuse over historical preservation.

Major MIDI is an ode to the past with an eye on the future: shaped by what came before, built for how musicians work now, and left open for whatever comes next.

None of this would have been possible without SpessaSynth, Dave Rossum, and Infrasonic Audio—thank you.

Core Workflow

The basic Major MIDI flow is simple:

  1. Put MIDI files on the SD card.
  2. Put one or more SoundFonts on the SD card.
  3. Load a song and SF2 from the front panel.
  4. Play, sync, loop, and mix from the module.
  5. Save song-specific state when the setup is dialed in.
